Primitive-Informed Sampling-Based MPC for Multi-Fingered Dexterous Manipulation

Anonymous submission
Paper Video

Low-dimensional manipulation primitives bias the sampling distribution of a sampling-based MPC, while joint-level residuals adapt the motion to the current hand–object configuration. On a physical Allegro hand this yields 100% success (86/86) on continuous in-hand rotation without palm support, where unstructured joint-space sampling never completes a single revolution, even with 6× more samples.

Abstract

We present a primitive-informed sampling-based model predictive control (MPC) framework for multi-fingered dexterous manipulation. Sampling-based MPC avoids complex gradient calculations through contact dynamics, but its effectiveness depends strongly on the distribution of sampled trajectories, due to the challenges in exploring the high-dimensional joint space.

Our framework biases sampling using low-dimensional manipulation primitives that encode coordinated finger motions, while simultaneously optimizing joint-level residuals to adapt these motions to the current hand–object configuration. Task-related rollout constraints reject infeasible trajectories during forward simulation, improving the effective use of the sampling budget.

We evaluate the approach on a physical Allegro hand using a synchronized MuJoCo digital twin. Ablations show that both the primitive and residual are necessary for reliable continuous in-hand rotation, that increasing the sampling budget alone does not recover this coordination, and that rollout constraints substantially improve success rate. A primitive extracted from a single object remains effective across object sizes and under model mismatch, and the framework further supports grasping, object reorientation, and coordinated arm–hand reach–grasp–transport, using primitives extracted from both a simulation-trained policy and human hand-motion data.

Method

The controller maintains a MuJoCo digital twin of the Franka arm, Allegro hand, and manipulated object. Robot encoders and motion capture update the twin at about 100 Hz, so every batch of candidate trajectories starts from the latest measured state, and commands are sent to the low-level controllers at 333 Hz. At each update, the cross-entropy method (CEM) samples candidate control trajectories represented by a small number of spline knots, rolls them out in MuJoCo, and refits the sampling distribution to the lowest-cost elites. Only the leading portion of the optimized trajectory is executed before replanning, yielding a receding-horizon feedback controller.

Directly sampling every joint command independently is inefficient because useful dexterous behaviors require coordinated motion across multiple fingers. We therefore sample in a primitive–residual latent space: a low-dimensional coordination basis extracted with PCA from example trajectories, plus a full-dimensional joint-level residual. Task constraints checked at every simulated step terminate infeasible rollouts early so they never enter the elite set.

System overview. A synchronized MuJoCo digital twin provides the state; CEM optimizes actions in the primitive–residual latent space subject to rollout constraints; primitives are extracted offline by PCA from a simulation-trained policy or from human hand-motion data (NinaPro).

Primitive–Residual Parameterization

At each spline knot the latent command is ηk = [zk, δqk], and the hand command is reconstructed as uh,k = q̄ + Φzk + δqk, where q̄ is the mean posture of the extraction data, the columns of Φ are d coordinated motion primitives, zk are their mixing coefficients, and δqk is the joint-level residual.

Fusing, not selecting. The coefficients in zk are optimized jointly, so multiple primitives contribute to the same knot. The primitive provides a structured low-dimensional direction for sampling; the residual prevents the optimization from being restricted to the primitive subspace.

Two-scale exploration. To avoid premature collapse of the CEM distribution, half of the candidates are sampled with an exploration standard-deviation floor and half with a smaller refinement floor, defined separately for the primitive coefficients and the residuals.

Primitive Extraction and Constrained Rollouts

Primitive extraction. Example joint trajectories exhibiting the desired behavior are temporally aligned and mean-centered, and PCA is applied to their covariance. The smallest number of principal components explaining at least 90% of the variance forms the basis Φ. The rotation primitive (4 components) comes from a SAC policy trained in simulation; the grasping primitive (5 components) comes from 25 subjects performing 20 grasps in the NinaPro human hand-motion database.

Task-constrained rollout evaluation. Constraints such as “the object must not touch the table” are checked after every simulated transition. A violating candidate is terminated immediately, assigned a large failure cost, and its rollout worker moves on to a new sample. This keeps infeasible trajectories out of the elite set and directs compute toward candidates that can contribute to the optimization.

Real-World Results

Experimental setup

All experiments run on a physical 16-DoF Allegro hand mounted on a Franka arm, with object pose from motion capture. The MPC uses a 0.5 s horizon with 6 spline knots and 10 elites per CEM update, and rollouts are evaluated in parallel on a 192-thread workstation (about 38 ms per update at N = 350 samples). We evaluate four tasks: continuous in-hand rotation (core ablations and robustness), object reorientation (isolates the rollout constraint), grasping (human-derived primitive), and coordinated arm–hand reach–grasp–transport. Unlike prior sampling-based MPC demonstrations with the palm facing up, the hand holds the object without palm support, so any sampling update that fails to find a grasp-maintaining motion results in a drop.

Experimental tasks: continuous in-hand rotation, object reorientation, grasping, and coordinated arm–hand reach–grasp–transport.

More samples do not replace the primitive

We vary the rollout budget N ∈ {100, 350, 600} for the full method and for a No primitive variant that samples directly in joint-command space, as in prior sampling-based MPC for dexterous hands.

  • No primitive completes zero rotations at every budget. Its maximum accumulated rotation plateaus at about 120–150°, the rotation achievable with the initial grasp. Going further requires finger gaiting, which independently sampled joint motions rarely produce, so a six-fold increase in samples does not recover the coordination.
  • Ours reaches 46.2% success (6/13) at N = 100 and 100% (86/86) at N = 350.
  • Too many samples hurt. At N = 600 the mean update time grows from 37.7 ms to 51.6 ms and success falls to 85.3% (64/75): slower replanning lets sim-to-real discrepancies accumulate, offsetting the benefit of extra samples. N = 350 is used for all subsequent hand-only experiments.

In-hand rotation performance versus sampling budget N. Top: rotation speed of successful rotations and success ratio for Ours and No primitive. Bottom: maximum accumulated rotation per attempt for No primitive. Mean MPC update time is shown below each N.

Ablations: primitive, residual, and constraint are all needed

At N = 350 we remove each component in turn. No primitive is additionally swept over three joint-space sampling scales (0.5, 1.0, 1.5 rad) to rule out a poorly chosen scale. No residual restricts all motion to the primitive subspace. No constraint disables the object–table contact constraint.

  • Both primitive and residual are necessary. No residual achieves 0/10 and No primitive achieves 0/30 across all sampling scales, versus 100% (86/86 over 10 three-minute trials) for the full method. The primitive supplies coordinated structure; the residual supplies the local flexibility to adapt it to the current hand–object configuration.
  • Constraints improve reliability, not speed. Disabling the rollout constraint drops success to 57.1% (72/126, p = 3.25×10-5), while the median speed of successful rotations is unchanged (20.42°/s without vs. 18.78°/s with, p = 0.604).

In-hand rotation ablations. Successful-turn rotation speeds for Ours and No constraint, and success ratios for these two conditions and the pooled No primitive / No residual ablations.

One primitive transfers across object sizes and model mismatch

Object size. Using the primitive extracted from the 40 mm across-flats hexagonal object without re-extraction, success remains 97.9% (93/95) on a 35 mm object and 98.4% (61/62) on a 45 mm object, versus 100% (86/86) on the 40 mm object. Drop counts (2, 1, and 0) are not significantly different (p = 0.34). Speed is unchanged for 35 mm and decreases from 18.78°/s to 13.17°/s for 45 mm (p = 0.046).

Model mismatch. Perturbing either the friction coefficient or the object mass in the digital twin by ±50% while leaving the physical task unchanged, the controller keeps rotating under all four conditions with success between 80.9% and 100%. Underestimating friction by 50% slows rotation to 13.03°/s (p = 0.002); underestimating mass by 50% is the only condition that significantly increases drops (18 over 30 minutes, p = 9.53×10-4).

Left: rotation across 40, 35, and 45 mm objects using the 40 mm primitive. Right: rotation under ±50% friction and mass mismatch in the MPC digital twin.

A human-motion primitive enables grasping

The hand starts open above the 40 mm object resting on the table, and the objective encourages lifting the object 10 cm while keeping its orientation. The five grasping primitives come from NinaPro human hand-motion data, with human flexion angles mapped directly to the Allegro index, middle, ring, and thumb flexion joints and the abduction–adduction joints driven only by the residual. The full method succeeds in 9 of 10 trials (lift ≥ 5 cm), while No primitive fails in all 10 (p = 1.19×10-4): without the primitive, sampled motions push or tilt the object rather than producing coordinated finger closure.

Grasping with a primitive extracted from human hand-motion data versus direct joint-space sampling.

Object reorientation: constraints reduce drops

A cyclic heading command traverses 60° over 10 s, holds for 3 s, and reverses. This task does not need finger gaiting, so direct joint-space sampling suffices and the effect of the rollout constraint can be isolated. With the constraint, 8 of 10 trials complete the full sequence, versus 3 of 10 without; the remaining trials end in drops that occur earlier and more often when the constraint is disabled. Tracking accuracy while the object is held is unchanged (median heading RMSE 7.87° vs. 8.26°, hold-period error 1.88° vs. 1.72°, both p = 0.993).

Left: desired and measured heading for a representative constrained trial. Right: active duration and outcome of each trial with and without the rollout constraint.

Coordinated arm–hand reach–grasp–transport

Each latent spline knot is augmented with a desired Cartesian end-effector pose for the Franka arm, with orientations perturbed in axis–angle form and interpolated by shortest-path slerp. The cost penalizes the object's world-frame pose error and its pose error relative to a desired object–wrist configuration; the budget is raised to N = 500. Starting with the hand away from the object, the controller establishes a grasp in 9 of 10 trials and transports the object to within 3 cm of the target in 8 of 10. The reach, grasp, and transport sequence is never programmed: it emerges from online optimization of the task objective. The supplementary video also shows a run in which the robot first rotates the object non-prehensilely, then grasps and transports it.
